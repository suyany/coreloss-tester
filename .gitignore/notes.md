The core measurement problem

A core-loss tester is a wattmeter: measure v(t) across a winding (∝ dΦ/dt by Faraday's law) and i(t) through it (∝ H), multiply and average to get real power. Almost all of that v·i product is reactive — energy moving into the field and back out, not dissipated — and the real loss is a small residual on top of it. Sullivan's survey gives this as fractional loss error ≈ Q·Δφ, where Q is how much bigger the reactive term is than the real term, and Δφ is the phase error between your measured v and i. Any timing mismatch between your two scope channels shows up as a fake Δφ. Because Δφ = 2πfΔt, the same absolute nanosecond of skew produces a worse error at higher frequency — this is the single reason the problem gets harder as you push toward 1 MHz, not a change in the electronics' capability.

Waveform shape and why it depends on topology

Resonant converters (LLC) filter the switched square wave through a resonant tank, so the current — and the flux it produces — comes out close to sinusoidal. Hard-switched, non-resonant converters (flyback, forward, PWM buck) apply the switched voltage directly to the winding; since flux is the time-integral of voltage, a square-wave voltage integrates to a straight-line ramp, so the core sees a genuinely triangular or trapezoidal B(t), not a smoothed sine. Steinmetz-family models (SE, GSE, iGSE) predict loss from this waveform shape — peak flux, frequency, ramp slope — and are approximations calibrated against sinusoidal data, extended with varying success to other shapes.

The zero-state / dead-time question — still open, pending Kirkby's reply

Two different things can be called a "zero-voltage interval," and it's not yet confirmed which one Ranjram meant: (1) dead time — the short (tens to hundreds of ns) gap inserted between switching a leg's two devices, purely to prevent shoot-through; voltage during it is clamped near a diode drop, not exactly zero, set by whichever body diode conducts; or (2) a deliberately controlled freewheeling state — e.g., in a phase-shifted full bridge, both legs parked at the same rail so the primary sees a true 0V, used on purpose to control duty cycle, and potentially much longer. In both cases, since v = N·dΦ/dt, zero voltage means dΦ/dt = 0 — flux holds flat at whatever value it reached, not reset to zero. (For current: v = L·di/dt, so zero voltage means constant current, not zero current — same math, current instead of flux. This is why current keeps circulating through a shorted/freewheeling loop with no voltage driving it, the same way a flywheel keeps spinning with no applied torque.)

The "relaxation effect" — still an open hypothesis, not sourced

Ranjram named "zero state voltages that can induce relaxation effects" as something sinusoidal excitation can't see. My first guess — classical magnetic after-effect/disaccommodation (domain walls settling over time) — turned out to be the wrong timescale when checked against sources: documented disaccommodation in ferrites unfolds over seconds to hours (PMC paper on MnZn ferrites, Fair-Rite's glossary), while a converter's zero-state dwell lasts nanoseconds to microseconds — six to nine orders of magnitude too fast. A more plausible (still unconfirmed) mechanism: decaying eddy currents from the immediately preceding transition, which don't vanish instantly when dB/dt drops to zero and dissipate real energy during the "flat" interval. This distinction matters because it decides whether the effect is shape-dependent (predictable by a Steinmetz-type model from waveform shape alone, the way triangular/trapezoidal loss already is) or dwell-dependent (structurally invisible to any model that only looks at shape, because such a model assigns zero loss whenever dB/dt = 0). Separately: even if it's dwell-dependent and a model can never predict it, a real electrical v-i measurement isn't necessarily blind to it — by energy conservation, real dissipation has to show up as some signal at the winding terminals — the open question there is whether your instrument has enough sensitivity to resolve a small anomaly sitting on a near-zero background, which is a different requirement than phase alignment.

Non-sinusoidal measurement is harder than "different shape" implies

A sine wave is smooth everywhere, so a fixed timing skew produces a uniform, well-behaved phase error — exactly what Sullivan's formula assumes. A switched waveform has sharp, near-step transitions right at the edge of any zero-state dwell, precisely where current is largest. A small channel skew there multiplies v and i from mismatched instants right where v is changing fastest, injecting a sharp, localized error at every transition rather than a smooth average one. Square/trapezoidal waveforms are mostly edges and flats, so this concentration effect dominates in a way sine waves — which have no edges — never expose.

Two existing approaches to the same underlying phase-error problem

Mingkai Mu's VT CPES thesis uses "reactive cancellation": electrically subtract an estimate of the dominant reactive component before computing power, so the same absolute skew produces a smaller relative error because you're computing loss on a much smaller residual signal. Covers both sinusoidal and non-sinusoidal (rectangular AC) excitation. It's a research methodology, not a packaged instrument. ETH Zurich's transient calorimetric method sidesteps the phase problem entirely by measuring heat directly (temperature rise × known thermal capacitance) rather than v and i — genuinely waveform-agnostic, validated to 5.0% average / 13.0% max deviation against electrical methods, tested to 500 kHz on MnZn ferrite. Also an unreplicated lab prototype requiring specialized thermal calibration (a DSC), not something "others can buy or use." Neither cleared your project's own "rethink the project" threshold when checked directly.

Real-world benchmarks gathered from the outreach itself

Chen's group (MagNet) recalibrates de-skew once before each long measurement sweep (a few hours), not continuously — a working, published system treats a few hours as an acceptable stability window. MagNet's actual dataset range is 50–500 kHz (not 10 kHz–1 MHz as originally assumed), sine/triangular/trapezoidal, with <20% max error and ~10% average error against reference. The COMPEL 2023 quote's underlying automated system is sine-only, 5–16 MHz, RF-amplifier-driven, reading 12.6–57% off manufacturer curves — so "no method above 1 MHz" always meant sine, and non-sinusoidal above 1 MHz remains the actual open target.
